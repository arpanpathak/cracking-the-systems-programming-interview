Distributed Systems from the Ground Up

Metrics and Monitoring: System Design

Every system in this book has been described as doing what its design says. A real system has also to be watched, since machines fail, software is released with defects, and traffic arrives in patterns that nobody foresaw. The people responsible need to know, at every moment, how many requests the system is receiving, how many of them are failing, how long they take, and how close each machine is to the limits of its memory and its disks. They need to see these quantities change over hours and weeks, and they need to be woken at night when one of them goes wrong. The system that provides for this is a monitoring system, and the numbers it collects are called metrics.

A monitoring system has a peculiar workload. It receives an enormous number of very small writes, since every service on every machine reports dozens of numbers every few seconds, and those writes never stop. Its data is of a single shape, a number with a time attached, which permits a degree of compression that no general database approaches. Almost nobody reads the data, and yet when it is read, during an incident, the answer is wanted immediately and may span months. And the system has a responsibility of an unusual kind. It is the means by which every other failure is detected, and it must therefore go on working when the things around it do not.

This chapter covers

  • what a metric is, and the 3 kinds in common use
  • time series, and why their number is the quantity to watch
  • whether services send their measurements or are asked for them
  • storing a number every few seconds for a year in 2 bytes a point
  • reducing old data to 1 point a minute, and what that loses
  • why an average of response times misleads, and what to keep in its place
  • alerts that wake somebody for the right reasons
  • keeping the monitoring system alive when everything else is failing

What a metric is

A metric is a number, measured repeatedly, that describes some aspect of a running system. 3 kinds account for nearly all of them, and they behave differently enough that it matters which is which.

A counter is a number that only ever increases: the requests served since the process started, or the bytes sent. Its value at a single moment is of no interest. What is wanted is its rate of change, obtained by subtracting 2 readings and dividing by the time between them. Counters have the great merit that nothing is lost between readings. If a reading is missed, the next one still includes everything that happened in the interval.

A gauge is a number that rises and falls: the memory in use, the length of a queue, the temperature of a processor. Here the value at a moment is the thing of interest, and whatever it did between 2 readings is unknown. A queue that filled and emptied within 5 seconds leaves no trace in a gauge read every 10.

A histogram records the distribution of a quantity that takes a different value on every occasion, above all the time taken to answer a request. Its treatment needs a section of its own, since the natural way of summarising such a quantity, by its average, turns out to be nearly useless.

Time series

A measurement is identified by the name of the metric and by a set of labels, which are pairs of a key and a value saying what the measurement refers to. The requests served by the payments service on machine 14 with status 500 might be recorded as the metric requests_total with the labels service=payments, machine=14 and status=500. The sequence of values recorded for 1 metric with 1 particular set of label values is a time series.

Labels are what make metrics useful, since they allow the same measurement to be added up or separated out along any dimension: all requests to payments, all requests with status 500, all requests on machine 14. They are also the principal hazard in the operation of a monitoring system, for a reason that follows from simple multiplication.

Every distinct combination of label values is a separate series, to be stored and indexed separately. A metric with the labels service, machine and status, in a system with 20 services, 100 machines and 5 statuses in use, has up to 10000 series, which is entirely manageable. Suppose now that a well-meaning engineer adds a label for the identifier of the user, so as to see which users experience errors. With a million users, the 1 metric has up to 10 billion series. The number of series that a metric gives rise to is called its cardinality, and a single label whose values are unbounded, such as a user identifier, a request identifier or the full text of an address, is enough to exhaust the memory of the system within hours.

The rule that follows is that labels must take a small and bounded number of values. A question about an individual user or request is a legitimate one, and it belongs to a different kind of system: to logs, which record individual events, or to traces, which follow a single request through the services it touches. Metrics answer questions about aggregates, and pay for their cheapness by being unable to answer any other kind.

Sending or being asked

Measurements have to travel from the services to the monitoring system, and there are 2 ways of arranging it.

Under the push model, each service sends its measurements to the monitoring system at regular intervals. Under the pull model, each service exposes its current values at an address of its own, and the monitoring system visits every service at regular intervals to collect them.

The second row is the strongest argument for pulling. When the monitoring system does the asking, the absence of an answer is information, and the health of every service is checked as a by-product of collecting its numbers. The third row is the strongest argument for pushing, since a batch job that runs for 5 seconds cannot wait to be visited. Many installations use both, pulling from long-lived services and accepting pushes from short-lived ones. The design of this chapter pulls, with collectors that visit each service every 10 seconds.

Storing a number every 10 seconds

The store receives points, each consisting of a time and a value, and must keep them for months. Stored plainly, as 2 numbers of 8 bytes, a point occupies 16 bytes. A million series sampled every 10 seconds produce 8.6 billion points a day, or 138 GB, and a year of that is 50 TB.

Time series have 2 properties that allow far better. The points of a series arrive in order of time and are never modified afterwards, so that the store only ever appends. And successive points resemble one another closely: the times are evenly spaced, and the values change little from one reading to the next. A number that is close to its predecessor can be stored as the difference between them, and a small difference needs few bits.

For the times, the differences are themselves nearly constant, the interval of collection, so that storing the difference between successive differences yields 0 almost every time. For the values, the difference from the previous value is small whenever the quantity being measured is steady, which most are for most of the time.

The simulation for this chapter stores an hour of a response time measured every second, 3600 points. Stored plainly they occupy 57600 bytes. Stored as changes, with small numbers written in fewer bytes, they occupy 7223, which is 2 bytes for each point and an eighth of the plain size. Production systems encode at the level of individual bits and exploit the structure of floating-point numbers, and commonly reach between 1 and 2 bytes a point. The 50 TB a year computed above becomes about 5.

Compression of this kind has a consequence for the layout of the store. A point encoded as a difference can be decoded only by starting from the beginning of its run and working forwards. Points are therefore grouped into blocks, each covering a fixed span of time for 1 series, 2 hours for example, and each block is encoded and decoded as a unit. The block being written is held in memory, where it can be appended to, and is protected by a write-ahead log of the kind described in chapter 2, and when its span is over it is compressed and written to disk, never to be modified again. Reading a range of time means locating the blocks that cover it, which is quick because blocks are indexed by series and by time.

Keeping less of the past

Even well compressed, points every 10 seconds for a year are more than most questions about the past require. A graph of the last year, drawn on a screen 2000 pixels wide, can show at most 2000 values, and to compute them from 3 million points for each series would be slow and pointless.

Older data is therefore reduced in resolution, a process called downsampling or rollup. A background job reads the raw points of each series and writes 1 point for every minute, and later 1 for every hour. Recent data is retained at full resolution for some days, the 1-minute data for some months, and the hourly data for years. A query chooses the resolution suited to the range it covers.

What is written for each minute has to be chosen with care, since a single number cannot represent 6 or 60 readings without losing something, and what is lost depends on the number chosen.

In the simulation, 1 minute contains 57 seconds in which the response time is about 40 ms and 3 seconds in which it is 900. Kept as its average, that minute is recorded as 83 ms, which suggests a slight slowness and conceals entirely that for 3 seconds the service was nearly unusable. Kept as its largest value, the minute is recorded as 900 ms, which preserves the incident and says nothing of how brief it was. Neither is wrong, and neither is sufficient. A rollup therefore stores several numbers for each interval, usually the smallest value, the largest, the sum and the count, from which the average can be derived and the extremes are not lost.

Why an average of response times misleads

The same example exposes a wider difficulty. The time taken to answer a request is the most important single measure of a service, and its average is a poor description of it.

Response times are not distributed symmetrically about a typical value. Most requests are fast, and a small proportion are very much slower, because they met a busy disk, an expired cache entry or a retry. The average is pulled upwards a little by these slow requests and describes neither group. A service whose requests take 40 ms in 99 cases and 2 seconds in the hundredth has an average of 60 ms, which looks entirely healthy, while 1 user in every 100 waits 2 seconds, and a user who loads a page that issues 50 requests has a chance of 2 in 5 of encountering at least 1 of them.

The quantity that describes experience is the percentile, introduced in chapter 1. The 99th percentile, written p99, is the time within which 99% of requests complete, and it states directly how bad the experience is for the least fortunate 1 in 100.

Percentiles have an awkward property that averages lack, and it shapes how response times must be collected. They cannot be combined. The average of 2 machines can be computed from the average and the count of each. The p99 of 2 machines cannot be computed from the p99 of each: if one machine has a p99 of 50 ms and the other of 2 seconds, the combined p99 may be almost anything between, depending on how the requests were divided. A service that computes its own percentiles and reports them has produced numbers that can never be aggregated across machines or across time.

The histogram solves this. A service does not report a percentile. It counts its requests in buckets by duration, so many under 10 ms, so many under 50 ms, so many under 250 ms and so on, and reports the counts. Counts can be added, across machines and across intervals, and a percentile is estimated from the combined counts at the moment of the query. The estimate is only as fine as the buckets, so the boundaries of the buckets have to be chosen around the durations that matter, but it can be computed for any grouping of machines over any period, which no percentile computed in advance allows.

Alerts

A monitoring system that is only looked at when something is already known to be wrong has done half its work. The other half is to notice the trouble first, by means of alerts. An alert is a rule consisting of a query and a condition, which the system evaluates at regular intervals, notifying somebody when the condition holds.

Rules are easy to write and hard to write well, and the commonest failing of monitoring in practice is too many alerts. An engineer who is woken 3 times a night by alerts that required no action soon ceases to respond with any urgency, and the alert that matters is then lost among the rest. A few principles keep alerts worth answering.

An alert should concern what users experience. What machines are doing is a different matter. “The proportion of failed requests has exceeded 1%” is a statement about harm. “Processor usage is at 90%” is a statement about a machine, which may be working exactly as intended. Alerts on causes multiply endlessly, since there are countless possible causes, while the symptoms that matter are few.

A condition should have to hold for some time before the alert fires. A single reading above a threshold is very often a momentary disturbance, and a rule that requires the condition to persist for 5 minutes ignores the noise at the price of 5 minutes’ delay.

Every alert should demand an action. If the proper response to an alert is to observe it and go back to sleep, it belongs on a dashboard, and nobody should be notified of it.

And related alerts should be grouped. When a database fails, 50 services that depend on it fail too, and 50 alerts arriving together tell the engineer less than 1 that names the database. The notification itself is delivered by the notification service of chapter 13, with escalation to a second person if the first does not acknowledge it.

One further case deserves mention because it is so often overlooked. A rule that tests whether a value is too high does not fire when there is no value at all. If a service stops reporting altogether, its error rate is not high, it is absent, and a naively written alert remains silent during the worst failure of all. Every important series therefore needs a second rule that fires when data has ceased to arrive.

Watching the watcher

The monitoring system is relied upon precisely when other things are failing, and its design has to take account of that.

It must not share the fate of what it monitors. If it runs on the same machines, the same network and the same storage as the services it watches, a single failure may remove both, and the engineers are left without information exactly when they need it most. It is therefore given infrastructure of its own, and kept as simple and as free of dependencies as its function allows. A monitoring system that needs the message queue, the coordinator and the object store all to be healthy before it can display a graph will be unavailable in most serious incidents.

It must also withstand the load that incidents themselves produce. When something breaks, many engineers open many dashboards together, each issuing dozens of queries over long ranges, at the very moment when the stream of incoming measurements may also be surging. The paths for writing and for querying are separated, so that a flood of queries cannot delay the storing of new points, and queries are bounded in the time and memory they may consume.

Finally, something has to watch the monitoring system itself. The usual arrangement is a second, minimal instance that checks nothing except whether the first is alive and evaluating its rules, and that raises its own alert through an independent channel when it is not.

Non-functional requirement 4 distinguishes 2 things that are often run together. The API

Under the pull model, a service takes no action to report. It answers a request from a collector with its current values. The API of the monitoring system itself is used by dashboards and by the people who define alerts.

What a collector receives from a service

GET /metrics HTTP/1.1
Host: payments-14.internal

HTTP/1.1 200 OK
Content-Type: text/plain

requests_total{service="payments",status="200"} 918234
requests_total{service="payments",status="500"} 417
queue_length{service="payments"} 12
request_duration_bucket{service="payments",le="0.05"} 880112
request_duration_bucket{service="payments",le="0.25"} 915540

Each line is the present value of 1 series. The lines of request_duration_bucket are a histogram: le is the upper bound of a bucket in seconds, and each count includes every request that took no longer than that.

One measurement, step by step

The steps follow the arrows of figure 24.5.

  1. Every 10 seconds, a collector asks each of its services for its current values.
  2. The collector attaches the time to each value and sends the points to the series store, which appends each to the current block of its series.
  3. A dashboard sends a query. The query service finds the blocks that cover the range, decodes them, and computes the result.
  4. In the background, the rollup job reads blocks that are some hours old and writes 1 point for each minute.
  5. Once a minute, the alert evaluator runs the query of every rule, and notifies the people named when a condition has held for long enough.

So that the loss of 1 machine of the series store leaves no gap, every point is sent to 2 machines, each of which builds its own blocks independently. The 2 copies are not kept identical by any protocol, and may differ by a point here and there. For this data that is acceptable, and the query service merges whatever it receives.

Summary

  • Metrics are numbers measured repeatedly, of 3 kinds: counters, gauges and histograms.
  • Every combination of label values is a separate series, and a label with unbounded values will exhaust the system.
  • Pulling lets the monitoring system notice a service that has fallen silent, and pushing suits jobs that live for seconds.
  • Points arrive in order and resemble their predecessors, so that storing each as its change from the one before takes about 2 bytes.
  • Old data is reduced to 1 point for each minute or hour, keeping the smallest value, the largest, the sum and the count.
  • An average of response times hides the slow requests that users notice, and percentiles must be computed from histograms because they cannot be combined.
  • An alert should concern what users experience, hold for some time before firing, demand an action, and have a companion that fires when data stops.
  • The monitoring system runs on infrastructure of its own, and is itself watched by something independent.
